Types of home care, explained

The same search — "home care" — hides very different needs. Find the one that matches your situation; each shows the typical cost to expect, and the estimator above will price it for your region.

Hourly visiting care

A carer visits at agreed times — help getting up, washed and dressed, medication prompts, meals, light housework, companionship. Visits are usually 30 minutes to a few hours. Best for: someone who is mostly independent but needs reliable help at set points in the day. Start small; hours can grow as needs change.

Typical cost
£26–£42 / hour

By region; UK average £32/hr (Homecare Association, 2026). Estimate, not a quote.

Live-in care

A carer lives in the home and is on hand across the day and night — personal care, meals, medication, mobility support and company, in familiar surroundings with routines, pets and possessions intact. Best for: someone who needs support throughout the day, is unsafe alone overnight, or wants to avoid a care home. Often cheaper than round-the-clock hourly visits.

Typical cost
£1,100–£1,200 / week

Typical starting range, 2026. Complex needs and couples cost more. Estimate, not a quote.

Dementia care at home

Specialist visiting or live-in support from carers trained in dementia care: consistent faces, unhurried personal care, medication routines, gentle prompting, and keeping the person safe without taking away independence. Best for: Alzheimer's disease, vascular dementia and other memory conditions where familiarity and routine matter more than anything.

Typical cost
£26–£42 / hour

Hourly, by region; live-in dementia care from the live-in range above. Specialist providers may charge a premium — ask. Estimate, not a quote.

Respite care

Short-term care that gives a family carer a break — a holiday, a hospital stay of their own, or simply rest. Booked as visiting care or a live-in placement for days or weeks. Best for: family carers who need time away without moving their loved one into a care home. Book ahead: good respite slots go early, especially in summer.

Typical cost
£26–£42 / hour

Hourly by region, or live-in from £1,100/week for full-week breaks. Estimate, not a quote.

End-of-life & palliative care at home

Care that focuses on comfort, dignity and pain control in a person's final weeks or months, at home, alongside district nurses and the GP. Includes personal care, medication support, night sitting and support for the family. Ask first about funding: end-of-life care is where NHS Continuing Healthcare fast-track funding most often applies — it can cover the full cost.

Typical cost
£26–£42 / hour

Hourly by region if self-funded; may be fully NHS-funded under CHC fast-track. Estimate, not a quote.

Care after hospital discharge

Short, intensive support to get someone safely home and settled after a hospital stay: help with washing, dressing, meals, medication and mobility while strength returns, usually reviewed after a few weeks. Good to know: hospitals and councils often arrange short-term reablement free for up to six weeks — ask the discharge team before paying privately.

Typical cost
£26–£42 / hour

Hourly by region if arranged privately; NHS/council reablement may be free. Estimate, not a quote.

Night care & sleeping nights

Two kinds: a waking night, where the carer stays awake and helps whenever needed (toileting, medication, reassurance), and a sleeping night, where the carer sleeps in the home but is there if something happens. Best for: people who wander, fall, or need help several times a night — and for family carers who simply need to sleep.

Typical cost
£26–£42 / hour

Waking nights are usually charged hourly by region; sleeping nights are often a flat nightly fee — confirm in writing. Estimate, not a quote.

How to check a provider's CQC rating (England)

There are 15,232 registered home care providers in England — and only 29.7% hold a current rating. Five minutes on the regulator's website tells you more than any brochure. In Scotland use the Care Inspectorate, in Wales CIW, in Northern Ireland the RQIA — the steps are the same.

Search the regulator's register

Go to the CQC's services-in-your-area search and enter the provider's name or your postcode. If a provider isn't on the register at all, walk away — providing personal care without registration is illegal in England.

Read the overall rating — and its date

Ratings run Outstanding, Good, Requires Improvement, Inadequate. Just as important: when was it given? A "Good" from years ago, or no rating at all, means the provider hasn't been inspected under current standards. Ask them directly when they were last inspected.

Open the full inspection report

The report scores five questions — is the service safe, effective, caring, responsive and well-led? Read what inspectors actually found about staffing, training and medication, not just the headline grade.

Ask the questions a rating can't answer

Will the same carers visit each time? What happens when a carer is sick? How are visits timed and recorded? What is the notice period to change or end the contract? Good providers answer all of these in writing.

Compare at least two written quotes

Take your request pack from the estimator above to two or three registered providers. Compare them against the benchmarks on this page — and be wary of any quote far below the £34.42/hr sustainable rate: corners get cut somewhere, and it's usually visit length.

Funding: check these before you pay anything

A large share of families pay in full when they didn't have to. Three checks, in this order, before signing with any provider.

1. Free council needs assessment

Free — anyone can ask

Contact your local council's adult social care team and ask for a care needs assessment. It's free, and savings don't affect your right to one. If the council agrees help is needed, a financial assessment follows: in England (2026/27), savings above £23,250 mean paying in full; below that, the council contributes. The value of the home is not counted when care is provided in that home. In Northern Ireland, your Health and Social Care Trust assesses instead.

2. NHS Continuing Healthcare

Can cover 100% of costs

If the main reason for care is a health need — complex medication, severe mobility problems, advanced dementia with challenging behaviour — the NHS may fund the entire package, at home, with no means test. It starts with a checklist screening, then a full assessment. Read the NHS guide to Continuing Healthcare and ask your ICB or GP to start the checklist. End-of-life care can be fast-tracked.

3. Attendance Allowance

£76.70 or £114.60 / week

A non-means-tested benefit for people over State Pension age who need help with personal care or supervision — you don't need a carer in place to claim, and it can be spent on care. Higher rate is for help needed day and night. Claim on GOV.UK — Attendance Allowance (2026/27 rates). In Scotland, claim Pension Age Disability Payment instead.

Frequently asked questions

How much does home care cost per hour in the UK in 2026?

The average price of hourly domiciliary (home) care in the UK in 2026 is £32 per hour, according to the Homecare Association. Where you live matters a lot: London and the South East typically run £38–£42 per hour, while the North, the Midlands and Wales are typically £26–£30 per hour. The Homecare Association's recommended minimum sustainable rate for 2026/27 is £34.42 per hour. All figures are estimates, not quotes.

How much does live-in care cost per week?

Live-in care — where a carer lives in the home — typically starts from about £1,100–£1,200 per week in 2026. That can work out cheaper than hourly care once someone needs support across most of the day and night, and far cheaper than two carers visiting around the clock. Complex needs, couples and some regions cost more. Figures are estimates, not quotes.

Will the council pay towards home care?

Possibly. Anyone can ask their local council for a free care needs assessment, whatever their savings. If the council agrees help is needed, a separate financial assessment decides who pays. In England in 2026/27, if the person receiving care has savings and capital above £23,250 they normally pay the full cost themselves; below that, the council contributes on a sliding scale. The value of the person's own home is not counted when the care is provided in that home.

What is NHS Continuing Healthcare and could it cover the full cost?

NHS Continuing Healthcare (CHC) is a package of care arranged and fully funded by the NHS for adults whose main need for care is a health need — a 'primary health need'. It can cover care at home in full, and it is not means-tested. Eligibility is decided by assessment (a checklist, then a full assessment using the Decision Support Tool), not by diagnosis. Ask your local integrated care board or a health professional to start a checklist if you think it may apply.

How do I check a home care provider's CQC rating?

In England, go to the Care Quality Commission (CQC) website, search for the provider by name or postcode, and open its profile to see its overall rating — Outstanding, Good, Requires Improvement or Inadequate — plus the date and detail of its last inspection. Only 29.7% of home care providers currently hold a current CQC rating, so if a provider has no rating, ask when it was last inspected and read the most recent report. In Scotland check the Care Inspectorate, in Wales Care Inspectorate Wales (CIW), and in Northern Ireland the RQIA.

What is Attendance Allowance and how much is it?

Attendance Allowance is a non-means-tested weekly benefit for people over State Pension age who need help with personal care or supervision because of a disability or health condition. For 2026/27 it is paid at £76.70 per week (lower rate) or £114.60 per week (higher rate, for help needed day and night), and it can be spent on care. You do not need to have a carer already to claim. In Scotland, Pension Age Disability Payment has replaced it.
