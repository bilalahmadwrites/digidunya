Proven hook formulas behind viral TikTok hooks & Reels

          
Every hook this generator produces is built on one of these tested patterns. Learn the formula and you can write viral hooks for any video, on any platform, in any niche.

          

            

              The Curiosity Gap
              
Tease the payoff without revealing it: Nobody talks about what happens after [topic]… The viewer's brain needs the loop closed, so they keep watching.

            

            

              The Mistake Call-Out
              
Name the error your audience is probably making: Stop doing [topic] like this. Nobody scrolls past a warning aimed at them.

            

            

              The Bold Claim
              
Lead with your most surprising result or opinion: [Topic] is lying to you. Agreement or outrage — both stop thumbs and drive comments.

            

            

              The Number Promise
              
Put a number in the first line: 3 [topic] tricks nobody teaches you. Numbers feel concrete, quick and worth finishing.

            

            

              The Direct Question
              
Ask something the viewer must answer mentally: Why does nobody get results from [topic]? A question in the brain is a video still playing.

            

            

              The POV Opener
              
Drop the viewer inside a scene: POV: you finally understand [topic]. Instant identification — the cheapest attention there is.

Why the first 3 seconds matter more than the whole video

          

            

              
3 sec

              
The window a viewer gives your video before the thumb moves. Your entire hook lives or dies inside it.

            

            

              
1 swipe

              
Every video competes with the next one a single swipe away. There is no “warming up” in a feed.

            

            

              
Watch time

              
The ranking signal TikTok, Reels and Shorts all weigh heaviest — and it is decided by whether the hook holds.

            

          

          

            
Short-form platforms don't distribute videos — they distribute watch time. When someone opens the app, the algorithm serves your video to a small test audience and watches one thing above everything else: do people stay, or do they swipe? A video that holds viewers past the first 3 seconds earns longer average watch duration and higher completion rates, and the algorithm answers by pushing it to a bigger audience. A video that loses people instantly gets quietly buried — no matter how good minute two was.

            
That makes the hook the highest-leverage 10 words in your entire content process. The hook's only job is to open a loop the viewer can only close by watching: a question, a warning, a shocking claim, a result shown before the method. Say it and show it — most first views happen with the sound low, so the same hook must land as bold on-screen text and as your first spoken line or voiceover.

            
The good news: hooks are a formula, not a talent. The patterns in this generator — curiosity gaps, mistake call-outs, number promises — work in gaming, fitness, food, motivation and every other niche, on TikTok, Reels and Shorts alike, because all three feeds run on the same human reflex: don't leave me hanging.

          

        


        
        

          
Viral hooks FAQ — TikTok, Reels & YouTube Shorts

          
The questions every short-form creator asks about hooks, answered straight.

          

            

              What makes a good hook for TikTok, Reels and Shorts?
              
A good hook creates an open loop in the first 1–3 seconds: it promises a payoff, asks a question the viewer wants answered, states a surprising claim, or names a mistake the viewer might be making. It speaks directly to one specific viewer (“you”), uses simple everyday words, and — most important — the video actually delivers what the hook promised. A hook that over-promises gets clicks once and kills your completion rate forever.

            

            

              How long should a video hook be?
              
Keep the spoken hook under 3 seconds — roughly 5 to 10 words. On-screen text hooks work best at 4 to 8 words, readable in a single glance without pausing. What must land inside those 3 seconds is the punch — the opening words that open the loop; a full hook line can run a second or two longer as a voiceover, as long as the tease itself hits instantly. If your hook needs a second sentence to make sense, cut it down: one idea, one loop, no warm-up.

            

            

              How many hashtags should I use on TikTok, Reels and Shorts?
              
Three to five relevant hashtags is the sweet spot on TikTok and YouTube Shorts. On Instagram Reels, 3–5 targeted hashtags (or up to 10–15 highly specific ones) beat long generic lists. Mix one broad tag (#fyp, #viral) with niche tags that describe the exact video — the generator above builds that mix for you per platform.

            

            

              Do hooks really decide whether a video goes viral?
              
The hook is the biggest single lever you control. All three platforms rank heavily on watch time and completion rate, and both are decided in the opening seconds. A strong hook lifts average watch duration, which tells the algorithm to push the video wider. A hook can't save a weak video — but a weak hook can bury a great one.

            

            

              Should the hook be spoken, on-screen text, or both?
              
Both, in the same first 3 seconds. Most short-form video is first watched with the sound low or off, so bold on-screen text carries the hook for silent viewers, while the spoken line or voiceover reinforces it for everyone else. Same words, two channels, zero missed viewers.

            

            

              Can I use the same hook on TikTok, Instagram Reels and YouTube Shorts?
              
Yes. A strong hook travels across all three platforms because all three reward the same thing: instant curiosity and watch time. What changes per platform is the caption style and hashtag mix — which is why this generator adjusts them when you switch the platform toggle.

            

            

              What hook styles work best for gaming videos?
              
Gaming feeds run on stakes and disbelief: near-miss clutches, impossible odds and instant challenges. “Watch what happens at 0:07”, “1 HP vs the whole squad” and “Nobody survives this jump” style hooks — moment-teases and challenge frames — consistently out-pull generic “check out my gameplay” openers. Pick the Gaming niche above and try the Challenge style.

            

            

              Can I use these hooks for YouTube titles and thumbnails?
              
Yes — a short, punchy hook line works just as well as a YouTube title or thumbnail text, because both jobs are the same: earn the click in one glance. For titles, trim the hook to its sharpest half so it stays readable in search results. For thumbnails, cut it down to 3–4 big words that tease the payoff without giving it away.

            

            

              What’s the difference between a hook and a call-to-action?
              
A hook opens the video — its only job is to stop the scroll and earn the next few seconds of watch time. A call-to-action closes the video — follow, comment, share, watch part two. Hook first, call-to-action last: swap them around and viewers leave before they ever hear the ask.

            

            

              How many hooks should I test for one video?
              
Test 3 to 5 hooks for the same video idea before you settle on one. Post the strongest versions with different opening lines and compare average watch time and completion rate after a day or two. The hook that holds viewers longest becomes your template for the next batch of videos on that topic.

            

            

              How do I write hooks for product and sales videos?
              
Lead with the problem or the result, never the product name. “This fixed my patchy beard in two weeks” out-pulls “Check out our new oil” every time. Show the outcome in the first frame, name who it’s for (“If you run a small bakery…”), and save the price and the buy link for the end — the hook earns the watch, the demo earns the sale.

            

            

              How do I test a hook using first-3-seconds retention?
              
Open your analytics and look at the audience retention graph: the drop in the first 3 seconds is your hook score. A healthy hook keeps roughly 65–70% or more of viewers past that point; a steep early cliff means the opening line, not the video, is the problem. Change one thing at a time — the words, the first frame, or the on-screen text — repost, and compare the cliff. Small lifts here compound across every video you publish.

            

            

              What hook mistakes should I avoid?
              
The big four: starting with “Hey guys” or your logo (burns the whole 3 seconds), burying the payoff so the video feels like clickbait, using vague teasers like “You won’t believe this” with nothing specific attached, and copying a trending hook word-for-word so it no longer matches your video. Specific beats clever — name the result, the number or the mistake, and deliver exactly what the hook promised.

            

            

              Can I use these hooks for live videos and webinars?
              
Yes — open a live with the hook in the first seconds, state the payoff and the one thing viewers will leave with, and repeat the hook when new viewers join, since live audiences arrive at different times.

            

            

              Can I use these hooks for podcast episodes?
              
Yes — use the hook as the cold open and the episode title, promise one clear takeaway, and restate it after the intro music so listeners who skip ahead still hear the promise.

            

            

              How do I write a strong hook without clickbait?
              
Promise only what the video actually delivers, use a specific number, result or curiosity gap from the real content, and avoid exaggerating the outcome, because misleading hooks raise clicks but hurt watch time and trust.

            

            

              Can I use the generator for LinkedIn posts?
              
Yes — hooks work for LinkedIn text posts and carousels too. Keep the first line under about 10 words, lead with a result or a contrarian take, and put the detail in the following lines, since LinkedIn shows only the first lines before the ‘see more’ cut-off.

            

            

              Can it write hooks for TikTok Shop / product demo videos?
              
Yes — lead with the problem the product solves or a before-and-after result in the first 2 seconds, then show the product. Avoid exaggerated claims the product cannot prove.

            

            

              Can it write hooks for email subject lines and newsletter openings?
              
Yes — subject lines work best under about 50 characters with one clear curiosity gap, and the opening line of the email should deliver on the subject line’s promise immediately.

            

            

              How do I write hooks for educational and how-to videos?
              
Lead with the exact outcome the viewer will get, or the mistake they are making right now — “You’re tying your laces wrong” or “Edit a full video in 6 steps.” Then promise the steps in plain order (“Step one takes ten seconds…”) so the viewer knows the payoff is coming and how the video will get there.

            

            

              Do hooks with a number or statistic work better?
              
Yes — a specific figure earns attention faster than a vague claim: “3 settings” beats “some settings,” and “I lost 4 kg in 30 days” beats “I lost weight fast.” One rule: the number must be real and backable. An invented stat is clickbait — viewers and platforms punish it, and one exposed fake figure costs more trust than the hook ever earned.

            

            

              How do I write a hook for a short paid ad (5–6 seconds)?
              
Fit three things into the first seconds: one problem, one product shot and one action. The viewer can skip at around 5 seconds, so the brand and the product must land before the skip point — name the pain, show the product solving it, and give the next step (“Tap to try it”) inside that same window. Anything important placed after the skip point is money spent on viewers who never saw it.

            

            

              How do I write hooks for a small local business or shop?
              
For a local shop, the winning pattern is place plus a specific reason to visit: name your town, neighbourhood or a nearby landmark in the first line, then attach one concrete offer, product or problem you solve for people close by. Nearby viewers stop because the video is clearly about them rather than a generic ad, and a same-day detail — fresh stock, today’s special, a repair done in an hour — gives them a reason to act now. Example hook: “Live near Model Town? This bakery’s morning batch sells out by 11 — here’s why.”

            

            

              How do I write hooks for job-search and career-advice videos?
              
Lead with the exact frustration or hidden hiring rule your viewer is stuck on, then promise one concrete fix they can use today — a CV line to rewrite, an interview answer to copy, or an application step they are skipping. Job seekers stay when the hook names their situation and the result, because it feels like advice aimed at them, not generic motivation. Example hook: “Your CV isn’t the problem — this one line is getting you rejected.”

            

            

              How do I write hooks for product unboxing and first-impression videos?
              
Start before the reveal: show the sealed box or keep the product half-hidden, and tease the one thing you expect to surprise, disappoint or impress you, holding the full reveal back a few seconds. Viewers stay to see whether their own guess matches your first reaction, and an honest verdict afterwards is what turns that curiosity into trust. Example hook: “I bought the cheapest version I could find — the last thing in this box shocked me.”

            

            

              How do I write hooks for news and current-affairs posts?
              
Lead with the single most important development and name it specifically — who, what and where — instead of a vague “big news” tease, because credibility is what makes people stop on news content. Add the time element when it matters (“in the last hour”, “this morning”) and refresh the hook as the story develops, or viewers who already know the ending will scroll straight past. Example hook: “The court just announced its verdict — here’s what changes for you today.”

            

            

              How do I write hooks for charity and fundraising appeals?
              
Open with one specific person or moment, not a general cause — a named need and a concrete figure stops the scroll far better than a bare “please donate”. Then show exactly what a donation does and state the target honestly, because trust is what decides whether an appeal gets shared or swiped past. Example hook: “$25 covers one child’s school supplies for a month — 47 children are still waiting.”

            

            

              What hooks work best for real-estate and property videos?
              
Property viewers decide in the first few seconds whether a home is worth their time, so open with the surprise — a striking price, a sought-after location, or one hidden feature nobody expects. Put that number or feature in the first 3 seconds and let the tour prove it, instead of starting with a slow walk up the driveway. Example hook: “This 4-bedroom house costs less than a studio flat — wait until you see the garden.”

            

            

              What hooks work best for fitness and health videos?
              
Fitness hooks work best when they show a real transformation or name the one mistake most beginners make, because viewers instantly check whether it applies to them. Keep every claim honest and realistic — a believable result in a believable timeframe earns saves and follows, while exaggerated promises destroy trust and watch time. Example hook: “The #1 mistake beginners make at the gym — fix it and feel the difference in two weeks.”

            

            

              What hooks work best for food and cooking videos?
              
Food hooks land hardest when they hit the senses or promise speed: the sizzle, the cheese pull, the first bite, or a finished dish in 10 minutes. Show the finished dish first, then rewind to how you made it — once viewers have seen the payoff, they stay to learn exactly how it was done. Example hook: “Crispy, golden and ready in 10 minutes — here’s the one-pan trick.”

            

            

              How do I write hooks for travel and vlog videos?
              
Open on the place itself or the moment nobody expected — a stunning view, a wrong turn, or a local surprise — instead of a slow intro or a day-by-day recap. Name the destination and the twist in the first 3 seconds so viewers who dream about that place, or fear missing it, stay to see what happens next. Example hook: “You won’t believe what’s hidden behind this waterfall — locals kept it secret for years.”

            

            

              How do I write hooks for wedding and event videography?
              
Start with the most emotional beat — the first look, a voice cracking during the vows, or the single best moment of the day — and save the build-up and chronology for after the hook. Wedding and event viewers scroll for feeling, not a timeline, so lead with a tear, a laugh, or a reaction shot in the first 3 seconds and let the story unfold from there. Example hook: “He hadn’t seen her yet — watch his face the second he turns around.”

            

            

              How do I write hooks for finance, money-saving and budgeting videos?
              
Lead with a real money number or a costly mistake your viewer is probably making right now — a specific amount saved, lost, or wasted stops the scroll far faster than generic advice. Keep the figure honest and show the breakdown quickly, because finance viewers stay for proof, not promises. Example hook: “I was losing $120 every month on this one mistake — check your account today.”

            

            

              How do I write hooks for coaches and consultants selling a programme or session?
              
Lead with the client’s problem and the transformation, not your credentials — buyers scroll past qualifications but stop for a result they want for themselves. Name exactly where they are stuck and where the programme takes them, and save your experience and testimonials for later in the video once they are already listening. Example hook: “Stuck at the same income for a year? Here’s the shift my clients make in week one.”

            

            

              How do I write hooks for musicians and artists promoting a new song, album or artwork?
              
Let the sound or the work hit first, then name it — open on the strongest chorus, drop or reveal before you say a single word about the release. A title or announcement in the first seconds tells viewers it is an ad, while the music or artwork itself makes them feel something worth staying for. Example hook: “I wrote this chorus at 2 a.m. — listen to what happens at 0:08.”

            

            

              How do I write hooks for an app or SaaS launch video?
              
Open on the painful problem your product removes, then show the result in seconds — never start with your logo, feature list or company story. Name the frustration in the first line, cut straight to the finished outcome on screen, and let the demo prove the promise before you ever explain how it works. Example hook: “Still chasing invoices by hand? Watch this one send itself in 4 seconds.”

            

            

              How do I write hooks for customer testimonial and review videos?
              
Open with the customer's own words or their exact result in the first 3 seconds, not your intro or sales pitch — a real quote in a real voice feels like proof instead of an ad. Follow it immediately with who is speaking and what specifically changed for them, because a named person with a concrete result is what turns casual praise into something viewers actually believe. Example hook: 'I was ready to quit — then this gave me my first 10 sales in a week,' says Priya, a first-time seller.

            

            

              How do I write hooks for before-and-after transformation videos?
              
Start on the 'after' — show the finished result in the very first frame so viewers instantly see what is possible and want to know how it happened. Then promise the 'before' story and the turning point in your next line, and only rewind to the start once that curiosity is locked in. Example hook: 'This is my kitchen today — wait until you see the leaking mess it was 30 days ago.'

            

            

              How do I write hooks for Q&A and AMA videos?
              
Lead with the single most surprising or most-asked question from your list and say it out loud in the first 3 seconds, instead of announcing that it is a Q&A video. Viewers stay because they want that one answer first, and you can stack the remaining questions behind it once they are hooked. Example hook: 'Everyone keeps asking if I actually make money from this — here is the honest answer.'
